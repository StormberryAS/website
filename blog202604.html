Disagreeing with the CEO

In Norway, you can disagree with your CEO in a meeting. It is expected. It signals engagement.

In many Asian business cultures, open disagreement with a senior, especially in front of others, is deeply uncomfortable. It is not cowardice. It is respect.

The consequences of misreading this? Norwegian managers who think their Asian colleagues are passive. Asian team members who think Norwegian managers are disrespectful.

Neither is true. Both are operating by the rules they were taught.

Cross-cultural leadership is about knowing which rulebook is in play - and being flexible enough to switch.

"We want to grow 30% this year." - Goal.

Bilingual in Communication Styles

Norwegians are among the most direct communicators in the world. This is a superpower. At home.

Abroad, it can read as cold, dismissive, or even rude. Especially in cultures where the relationship must come before the transaction.

I've seen Norwegian executives lose deals in Asia and Latin America not because their product was wrong, but because they skipped the small talk, got straight to the agenda, and wondered why the room went quiet.

The fix isn't to be fake. It's to be bilingual in communication styles. Direct when the culture expects it. Relational when it doesn't.

This is a learnable skill. And it pays for itself on the first international deal.

Strategy is a Decision Filter

Most strategy documents are too long, too vague, and forgotten by Q2.

Here is the format we use at Stormberry with clients:

  • Where are we now? Honest assessment; strengths, real weaknesses.
  • Where do we want to be? Specific, time-bound.
  • How will we get there? 3 strategic moves, not 12.
  • What will we NOT do? This is the hardest and most important line.
  • How will we know we're winning? 2-3 measurable signals.

One page. Shared with everyone. Revisited quarterly.

Strategy is not a document. It's a decision filter.

15/April/2026

The Reality of Direct Communication

When I moved from Brazil to Norway, I thought I understood direct communication. I was wrong.

In Brazil, "direct" means I'll tell you what I think, but wrapped in warmth, context, and relationship. In Norway, direct means: here is the fact. Full stop.

Neither is better. But misreading one for the other can cost you a deal, a partnership, or a team member.

High-context cultures (Japan, Brazil, much of the Middle East) communicate through implication, relationship, and what is NOT said. Low-context cultures (Norway, Germany, Netherlands, USA) communicate explicitly: what is said is what is meant.

The skill is knowing which room you're in.
